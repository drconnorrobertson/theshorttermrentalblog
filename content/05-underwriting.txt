What is short-term rental underwriting?|Underwriting is the process of testing whether an accommodation plan makes financial sense under explicit assumptions. It connects available nights, pricing, occupancy, operating expenses, setup cash, financing, and reserves. A useful model shows both favorable and unfavorable outcomes. It should distinguish observed evidence from estimates and avoid hiding uncertainty behind a single precise revenue figure. Update it when actual operating results become available.|Build a monthly model with base, lower-revenue, and delayed-launch scenarios.
How do I estimate accommodation revenue?|Multiply expected booked nights by the average accommodation rate actually earned, using consistent definitions. Build the estimate month by month when demand is seasonal. Cleaning charges, taxes, platform fees, and refunds should be handled explicitly rather than mixed into an unclear gross number. The result is an estimate, not a commitment from future guests. Keep the availability and pricing assumptions visible.|Define exactly which charges your revenue line includes.
What is average daily rate?|Average daily rate, often called ADR, measures accommodation revenue per booked night over a stated period. Divide the relevant accommodation revenue by booked nights. Mixing cleaning fees or taxes into the numerator can distort comparisons. A higher ADR does not necessarily create more total profit if booked nights fall or service costs rise. Compare it with occupancy and retained cash using the same dates.|Write the numerator and denominator beside every ADR figure.
How do I calculate occupancy?|Divide booked nights by the available nights defined for the analysis. Some comparisons use all calendar nights, while others exclude owner or maintenance blocks. These approaches can produce different percentages for the same home. State the denominator so another person can interpret the figure. Occupancy alone does not measure profitability, especially when bookings are won through heavy discounts.|Report both booked nights and the availability definition.
What is revenue per available night?|Revenue per available night divides accommodation revenue by the chosen available-night denominator. It combines the effects of nightly rate and occupancy, making it useful for comparisons with consistent definitions. It still excludes the expense side of the business. A strong figure can coexist with weak cash flow when cleaning, management, financing, or maintenance costs are high. Use it alongside actual retained cash.|Compare identical periods and availability assumptions before interpreting changes.
What expenses belong in a rental model?|Include the expenses needed to deliver and sustain the accommodation: utilities, suitable insurance, cleaning, supplies, maintenance, management, software, platform charges, taxes or fees you bear, and other property-specific obligations. Model debt payments and capital replacements separately where appropriate to your reporting framework. Omitting small recurring expenses can materially overstate retained cash over a year. Use actual quotes and records when available.|Maintain a line-item expense list and mark estimates separately from verified amounts.
Is gross booking value the same as profit?|No. Gross booking value may include accommodation charges, cleaning, taxes, or other amounts depending on the system's definition. Profit requires accounting for relevant expenses, and cash retained also depends on payments, financing, and capital spending. Two dashboards can display different gross figures for the same reservations. Reconcile the definitions before comparing performance or using a seller's report to support a purchase.|Trace one reservation from guest total to payout and then to related costs.
How do I calculate cash flow after debt?|Start with actual or projected cash inflows and subtract the cash outflows required during the same period, including operating costs and debt payments. Include relevant replacement spending and distinguish reserve transfers from expenses. Avoid mixing future bookings with cash already earned. Your calculation should explain what is excluded, such as personal income taxes. Consistency makes the model useful for month-to-month decisions.|Show operating cash, debt payments, and capital spending on separate lines.
What is a break-even occupancy calculation?|A break-even occupancy calculation estimates the booked nights needed to cover specified costs at an assumed rate and contribution per stay. Include variable costs rather than treating every additional night as pure margin. The result changes with stay length, cleaning economics, discounts, and fixed obligations. A single occupancy target is therefore conditional on the model's assumptions, not a universal benchmark.|State the rate, variable-cost assumptions, and costs covered by the calculation.
How do I account for cleaning fees?|Treat guest cleaning charges and cleaner payments as separate lines so you can see whether turnover economics work. A fee can differ from the actual cost, and stay length changes its effect on the total guest price. Include laundry, supplies, inspection time, and any charges you bear. Do not assume that charging a cleaning fee makes cleaning financially neutral or removes it from your operating responsibilities.|Calculate the net cleaning contribution for a typical reservation.
How should I model property management costs?|Use the actual proposed contract rather than a generic percentage. Check the fee basis, additional charges, minimums, maintenance coordination, marketing costs, and included tasks. Some fees may apply to revenue categories you did not expect. Model the cost alongside the work it replaces and the obligations you retain. A lower headline percentage is not necessarily a lower total operating cost.|Request a worked example for one representative reservation and month.
What is a sensitivity analysis?|Sensitivity analysis changes selected assumptions to show how the result responds. For a rental, useful inputs include nightly rate, booked nights, repair costs, management fees, and financing payments. Change one input at a time to understand its effect, then test combined downside cases. This reveals where the plan is fragile. It does not assign probabilities unless you have evidence supporting them.|Identify which two assumptions most strongly affect retained cash.
How should I model an opening delay?|Move the first revenue date and keep the cash obligations that continue before launch. Add any extra work, rescheduling, or carrying costs caused by the delay. Do not merely reduce annual occupancy while leaving setup timing unchanged. A launch delay can consume reserves before any guest arrives. Model a realistic delay explicitly so your acquisition budget accounts for it.|Create a separate pre-opening cash schedule with no guest revenue.
What is cash-on-cash return?|Cash-on-cash return compares a specified annual cash-flow measure with the cash invested. The definition of both inputs matters, including whether setup, reserves, debt payments, and replacement spending are included. It is not the same as total investment return and does not capture every risk or appreciation outcome. Use it to compare consistently defined scenarios rather than as a standalone purchase signal.|List the cash invested and the exact annual cash-flow numerator.
How do I model owner stays?|Remove owner-use dates from guest availability and account for any cleaning or other incremental costs. Peak-season owner stays can affect revenue more than quiet-season use. Tax and accounting effects require separate analysis for your circumstances. Do not use a full-year availability forecast while simultaneously planning personal use. Keep owner dates visible so the forecast and actual calendar can be reconciled.|Run the model with your intended owner dates blocked.
How should I budget maintenance reserves?|Estimate recurring service work and likely replacement needs based on the property and its condition. Use inspections, service records, and contractor input where relevant. A generic percentage may be a rough starting assumption but should not replace property-specific planning. Reserves support future cash needs; they do not make an expense disappear. Review the target when equipment changes or repair history reveals a new pattern.|Create a replacement list and a separate routine-maintenance budget.
How do discounts affect an underwriting model?|Discounts reduce the rate actually earned and may change booking volume or stay length. Model the realized price rather than the displayed standard rate. Separate launch promotions from the recurring pricing assumption. A strong occupancy figure supported by heavy discounts can overstate the appeal of your normal price. Compare the additional contribution from bookings with the revenue given up on nights that might otherwise have sold.|Record promotion dates and calculate revenue at the actual discounted rate.
How should I handle cancellation assumptions?|Forecasts should distinguish reserved nights from completed stays and allow for uncertainty around cancellations and refunds. Use your actual terms and operating history when available rather than assuming every reservation converts to earned revenue. Model cash timing separately, since payouts and refunds may occur in different periods. Check platform policies for current details. Cancellation risk becomes especially relevant when a few high-value bookings dominate the plan.|Track booked, canceled, completed, and refunded reservations separately.
Can I annualize one strong month?|Annualizing one strong month can misrepresent a seasonal business. A peak month may reflect events, holidays, promotions, or a small number of unusually valuable stays. Build a twelve-month model and compare evidence from the full seasonal cycle. If you lack historical data, label the estimates clearly and use ranges. The purpose is to understand year-round obligations, not create the largest possible headline revenue.|Estimate quiet months explicitly before calculating the annual result.
How do taxes collected from guests affect revenue reports?|Taxes collected from guests may be amounts owed to an authority rather than money retained by the operator. Systems handle these amounts differently, and collection responsibilities vary. Distinguish guest totals, amounts remitted by platforms, amounts you must remit, and actual accommodation revenue. Discuss accounting treatment with your adviser. Including every guest-paid amount as spendable income can overstate cash available for operations.|Reconcile one reservation's tax handling and maintain a separate liability record where applicable.
What does a downside scenario include?|A useful downside combines plausible adverse conditions such as lower revenue, longer vacancy, an opening delay, and unexpected repairs. It should be specific enough to calculate cash needs and decision thresholds. Avoid a token small reduction that leaves the core optimistic assumptions intact. The scenario does not predict the future; it tests how much room the plan has when conditions disappoint.|Describe the downside in plain language before entering its numbers.
How do I compare short and longer rental stays financially?|Compare the revenue and costs for the same calendar period. Short stays can create more turnovers, messages, and transaction costs; longer stays may change rates, utilities, vacancy patterns, and legal obligations. The correct comparison depends on the property and lawful operating options. Use actual quotes and plausible guest demand rather than assuming one duration is always more profitable.|Model turnover frequency and total occupied nights for each lawful option.
Should appreciation be necessary for the plan to work?|Property appreciation is uncertain and should be separated from operating cash flow. A rental may have objectives beyond income, but relying on a future sale price can hide current cash strain. Evaluate whether you can support the property under its actual obligations. If appreciation appears in an investment scenario, label the assumption and include transaction costs and unfavorable outcomes rather than treating the gain as earned.|Assess the operating plan without assumed price growth.
How do I reconcile a forecast with actual results?|Use the same definitions and periods for forecast and actual figures. Separate price changes, occupancy changes, availability changes, and expense differences. Investigate the causes rather than replacing the original model silently. A variance can reveal either an inaccurate assumption or an execution problem. Keep a version history so your next purchase or operating decision benefits from what the first model got wrong.|Review monthly differences and write one action linked to each material variance.
What makes a rental spreadsheet trustworthy?|A trustworthy spreadsheet has visible assumptions, consistent definitions, traceable sources, clear formulas, and checks that totals reconcile. It distinguishes revenue, operating costs, debt payments, capital spending, and reserves. Hidden hard-coded values and unlabeled exclusions make a model difficult to assess. Test edge cases such as zero bookings or a delayed launch. The spreadsheet should communicate uncertainty rather than imply precision it cannot support.|Have another person trace one month from assumptions to final cash result.
